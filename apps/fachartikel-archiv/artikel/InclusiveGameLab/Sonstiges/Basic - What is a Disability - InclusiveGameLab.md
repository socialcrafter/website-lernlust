# Introduction

Let\'s start with the basics: What is a disability? How would *you* define it?

To put it simply: A disability is a mismatch between a person and their environment.

For example: Someone using a wheelchair is not disabled because of a medical condition or because they move around in a wheelchair -- on the contrary, wheelchairs provide mobility and freedom to people with motor impairment\... that is, until these people encounter a barrier such as a flight of stairs which is the only way to access a building.

# The Social Model

What I just described to you is the so-called social model of disability.\
\
Unlike the medical model, which was prevalent until the early 2000s, the social model does not identify a medical condition as the root of a disability.\
\
Instead, it acknowledges that these medical conditions -- or impairments -- do impact the everyday lives of individuals, but the disabling factor is actually an environment that is not designed with them in mind. In addition, it accounts for so-called attitude-based barriers, for example biases, stereotypes, and exclusionary behaviours that can have a strong negative impact on the everyday lives of people with impairments.

There are three main types of impairments: sensory, cognitive, and motor impairments.

Sensory impairments refer to the ability, or lack thereof, to perceive acoustic or visual stimuli. This means that players with sensory impairments may struggle with *perceiving* information, especially when this information is only delivered through one sensory channel (so: audio *or* visual).

Cognitive Impairments cover a wide range of conditions related to cognitive processes and mental health. While playing games, people with cognitive impairments most often encounter barriers in the *perception and processing* stage of gaming

Motor impairments affect a person's ability to move, touch and feel. As you can probably guess, games may exclude players with unintentional barriers at the *input* stage.

It's important to keep in mind that:

\...many of the aforementioned are invisible impairments, which can have as strong an impact on a person's life as any other disability.

\...there are huge differences between disabled individuals, even if they belong to the same „disability group".

\...and individuals can have multiple impairments, which makes finding accessibility solutions more challenging, or example when a visually impaired gamer with autism is faced with increased visual and acoustic stimuli, which may lead to overstimulation. In addition, since games usually require a much broader range of physical and cognitive activity than, for example, movies, there's a higher risk of encountering such unintended barriers All of this means that removing these barriers may require entirely different accessibility strategies, **there is not „one size fits all" solution!** But don't worry! There are actually many different approaches to tackle this problem, some of which you'll learn in later lessons.

First, let's learn more about disabilities, because this knowledge is key to developing access solutions.

What is considered to be a disability strongly depends on culture and societal norms:

for instance, most people wearing glasses do not consider themselves to be disabled, despite being visually impaired, and glasses are more often treated as accessories instead of disability aids. That's because minor visual impairments like near-sightedness are ***normalised*** in our society; we have collectively agreed that these are natural variations in our bodies, and there's a range of accommodations readily available in many parts of the world. But despite being very common as well, impairments such as chronic fatigue, spinal disorders, ADHD, or depression are treated very differently. That's something to keep in mind!

Going back to the medical and social model, these two very different concepts lead to distinct design paradigms:

The medical model considers the impaired individual to be flawed. So accessibility endeavours designed around this model try to "compensate" for these flaws by offering optional support, in games this is usually by done providing options, for example high-contrast mode or optional screen reader support. There are two core issues with this approach:

First, it states that certain bodies are "normal" or "able", while impaired bodies deviate from this stated norm. They are not able, and need to be treated differently. This idea is where harmful phrases such as "special needs" or "handicapped" originate from. Through this line of thinking, certain individuals are othered and excluded. Secondly, it suggests that accessibility is a highly specialised discipline that only benefits a certain demographic -- which is not actually true, and we'll get to that in a moment.

The social model, on the other hand, emphasises that it's inaccessible *environments* that actively disable people. So design principles based on this model, such as universal design, acknowledge that environments can be made accessible, by default, for a large group of people, if certain aspects are considered in the design process right from the beginning. It also emphasises that universal design benefits not only people with impairments, but everyone.

Here\'s an example: subtitles are very important for players who are deaf or hard of hearing, because they cannot access information provided through audio channels -- or only to a limited extent. But subtitles are **also incredibly helpful for players\
who** are playing in a loud environment, players who have a different native language than what is being spoken in the game, or simply for parents who want to enjoy their favourite game with a sleeping baby nearby (so they have to disable or lower the volume of the sound).

This is called the \"Curb Cut Effect\" -- can you guess why? Maybe take a moment to think about this or discuss it with your class.

Curb cuts were originally introduced in city planning to make it easier for people with wheelchairs to cross streets. But soon after, it became obvious that they not only benefited this particular target group, but many others as well -- for example parents with buggies, cyclists or package deliverers. So the „Curb Cut Effect" describes that by removing a barrier for one group, you might additionally remove unintentional barriers for other people, or simply make their experience more pleasant. So by designing your game with accessibility in mind from the start, you not only make it available to more gamers with impairments, but you also help other people to access and enjoy your game.

And this is a good opportunity to highlight another important concept of disability: The Persona Spectrum. When thinking or talking about disabilities, we usually think about permanent impairments that affect people's lives forever. But there are also temporary and situational impairments. Let's take motor impairments as an example: a permanent motor impairment could be chronic pain or an amputated leg; the person affected uses a wheelchair. A temporary impairment could be an injury like a broken leg; it will heal over time, but for a while, the person affected will need crutches or a wheelchair as well to move. And finally, a situational impairment could result from a parent pushing a stroller, limiting their mobility if the environment is not barrier-free

So instead of creating a singular persona to design your product or game for, instead think of a persona spectrum: all users who will benefit from removed barriers.

There are many good reasons to rethink social ideas of disability, and make accessibility a design goal.
