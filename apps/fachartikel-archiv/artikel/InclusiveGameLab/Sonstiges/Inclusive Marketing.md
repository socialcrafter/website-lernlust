# Introduction

In this talk, we'll talk about making your marketing more accessible. This is especially important when promoting a game that is accessible by design or has great features people can use to adapt the experience to their needs. It would be a pity if people who could actually play the game would never hear about it, wouldn't it?

# Promoting accessibility

Make sure to share information on the accessibility of your game as early as possible. It's important for players with disabilities to know in advance whether they will be able to not only access, but also enjoy your game. Don't make them wait or search for this crucial information or you will risk losing players -- make it as easy to find as possible!

Treat it as a core feature of the product and market it as such. Consider highlighting it at showcases, in social media posts, press releases, and interviews. Ideally, set up a separate page on your website or add an appropriately headlined paragraph to official announcements. Also consider adding this information to every store page.

Accessibility is an asset you should market! Not only will you get the attention of players who need accessibility considerations to be able to play, it's also good PR. Accessibility features are of course valued by gamers who identify themselves as disabled, but able-bodies players are also increasingly aware that they can benefit from them, and may look out for games with such features.

Next, let's talk about what to do to make your marketing as accessible as possible.\
\
We'll start with some inexpensive, very easy to implement options and end with more challenging and costly ones.

# Marketing advice

First things first: do your best to avoid discriminatory language, which includes ableist words. Don't use terms like "lame", "crazy", "insane" or "blind spot". There are many alternatives, as languages around the world -- including English, which is most commonly used for games marketing -- are rich sources of expression. If possible, reach out for gamers or consultants with disabilities and check in if the terms you are using are appropriate.

Also watch out for language that may be used with good intentions, but many people with impairments strongly dislike, such as: "handicapped", "differently abled", or "special needs". Instead use the terms "disability", "impairment", "disabled", or "person with a disability".

## Social Media (low effort)

When it comes to your social media presence, carefully consider which platforms you want to use to promote your work, and why. Ideally, you should be present on several different platforms to reach as large an audience as possible. But put some thought into the following questions:

Who is your main target audience?

How does each platform work?\
Does it employ algorithms?\
Which features does it offer to make your posts more visible?\
What do you have to do to ensure they stay visible?

And perhaps also consider factors such as the political belief system behind the platform. Does it align with yours? If not, should you still support it, and if so: Why? This is particularly important if you want to promote diversity and inclusion.

Next, limit usage of emojis in social media posts. While these are very common nowadays and may bring the attention of sighted customers to your content, they don't work well with screen readers. And speaking of enjoyable online experiences: Add alt texts to all images! It's just a bit of additional effort and makes a BIG difference for visually impaired users.

What to keep in mind when writing alt texts: try to focus on the most important aspects of each image. Don't describe every little detail, because otherwise it will take too long to have these texts read out loud. Ideally, an alt text should not be longer than one or two sentences.

To give you an example, the description of this image could be: a photo of a red PlayStation 5 DualSense controller on a white blanket.

Depending on the platforms you're using, alt text implementation may be different, so\
make sure to look up guides, which are often provided by the platforms themselves.

When using hashtags, use capital first letters in every word so that people with cognitive disabilities and those using screen readers can understand them better. For example: #AccessbilityMatters instead of #accessibilitymatters.

Avoid special display characters, because screen readers can't read those properly. In addition, refrain from using stylised fonts, because these are much more difficult to read for people with dyslexia, cognitive disabilities, as well as visually-impaired and legally blind users.

Also, don't overly rely on GIFs, as they can pose significant challenges for some users with disabilities, especially blind users and people with cognitive impairments. If you're using GIFs, add alt text to those as well and make sure your GIFs do not contain blinking and flashing because these effects could be triggers for photosensitive epilepsy, or just cause discomfort.

## Press Releases (low effort)

As with all social media posts, you should add alt texts or descriptions below all images. Make sure to use headlines, and use them properly, which means: prioritise usefulness over aesthetics. Users with screen readers rely on headlines for navigation, so make sure not to "mix things up" just because it's more visually appealing to you. For example: having heading one followed by heading 4, followed by heading 2 would be very confusing for a blind user with a screen reader.

## Content Creators (low effort)

Reach out to content creators, and especially to content creators with disabilities, and provide them with a review copy. Acknowledge their expertise and, if possible, offer appropriate payment. If they play your game on stream or publish a review, your game will likely get immediate attention from the target group you're trying to address. Remember: high accessibility in games is still relatively uncommon, so you have a good chance to stand out!

Also, get in touch with advocacy groups like "AbleGamers" or the International Game Developers Association Game Accessibility Special Interest Group and tell them about your project. They might be interested!

## Websites (medium effort)

Take great care in making your website accessible. Screen reader support is vital, which includes adding proper headlines to facilitate navigation. Add alt text to all images and consider using simple language by default, or offer a simple language option for people with cognitive impairments.

## Social Media (medium effort)

Listen to and engage with your audience: take feedback seriously, show appreciation, and consider making changes in future versions if players, and especially players with disabilities, provide detailed feedback. First, this feedback is valuable and you such treat it as such. Second, you'll show your player base that you care about them and that is particularly important for this specific target group, which has been neglected for too long.

If you made mistakes and people highlight them\... just own it. Don't desperately try to defend your design decisions unless you have really, really good reason to. It's much better for your reputation to acknowledge shortcomings and promise to do better in the future. People *will* notice how you react in these situations.

## Trailers (medium effort)

Always add subtitles or, better yet, captions to your trailers, ideally in different languages. If you don't have the necessary resources, automated subtitles do the trick, as well. Though you have to be aware that they will likely not be entirely accurate and thus misrepresent parts of your carefully created trailer.

## Interviews and Podcasts (medium effort)

When sharing audio-only content like podcasts, always provide a transcript and/or subtitles -- or ask the interviewer to do it for you. If you don't have the time and resources to transcribe the audio add subtitles manually, you can use software to automate the process or upload the files to YouTube and enable auto subtitling (though be mindful that, again, automated subtitles are prone to errors).

## Trailers (high effort)

Audio description is increasingly common, especially for promoting AAA games. It means adding an optional audio track in which the most important visual details are described.
